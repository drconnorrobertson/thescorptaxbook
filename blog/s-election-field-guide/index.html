Election analysis and timing

How Accountants Analyze an S Corporation Election

Check eligibility, compare owner pay and total costs, then establish the effective date and filing route. A completed form is only one part of the decision.

Originally reviewed September 23, 2026Substantively revised November 22, 2026By AE Tax Advisors

An S corporation election deserves two separate decisions: whether the entity can elect and whether the expected result is worthwhile for its owners. An accountant then checks whether the requested effective date can be supported and who will implement payroll, bookkeeping, and return preparation. Revenue alone, a standard salary percentage, or a promised tax saving does not answer those questions.

Use the blank election-analysis review record to collect the inputs, source documents, reviewer, and unresolved items. This guide explains the review sequence; it does not select an entity or salary for a particular business.

Start with the current entity and its owners

Identify the legal entity, current federal tax classification, tax year, ownership history, and requested effective date. An LLC is a legal form, while S corporation treatment is a federal tax election. Do not assume changing a name or obtaining an EIN makes an election effective. For an existing C corporation or a business changing classifications, ask the preparer to review transition consequences separately rather than assuming the same result as a new sole-owner business.

The IRS S corporation overview lists the principal requirements: a domestic corporation, allowable shareholders, no more than 100 shareholders, one class of stock, and no disqualifying corporation status. Shareholder and stock-class rules need review of the actual ownership and governing documents. Certain trusts and estates can qualify; an owner being an individual or trust does not end the analysis. Obtain the required shareholder consents and identify ownership changes before preparing the filing.

Compare the same business under both tax treatments

Give the accountant a profit forecast that identifies what is already deducted. “Profit before owner compensation” and “profit after owner payroll” are different starting points. Reconcile the forecast to the books and specify which owner services, benefit costs, employer payroll taxes, and professional fees are included. Subtracting an expense twice can manufacture a favorable comparison.

A useful comparison keeps operating assumptions consistent and separately shows:

  • Current-classification federal and state tax results for the business and owners.
  • Proposed owner wages, employer and employee payroll taxes, and remaining business income.
  • Income-tax effects, applicable QBI treatment, benefits, and other return-level limitations.
  • Incremental payroll administration, bookkeeping, business-return preparation, state charges, and setup costs.
  • Cash available to the business and owners, the first-year result, and recurring annual costs.

These are requested model outputs, not amounts established by this guide. Ask which inputs are documented, estimated, or missing. Keep a partial cost subtotal separate from the final comparison. There is no universal profit threshold at which every business should elect.

Review owner compensation before estimating distributions

The IRS compensation guidance explains the reasonable-compensation requirement for shareholder-employees providing services before non-wage distributions. The analysis considers actual duties and circumstances; it is not established by a fixed wage-to-distribution ratio. Use the reasonable-compensation guide and its supporting workpaper before entering a salary into a tax projection.

A residual profit amount is not automatically spendable or tax-free cash. Review distributions and shareholder basis separately in the stock and debt basis guide. For the QBI calculation, section 199A excludes reasonable compensation from qualified business income. The QBI guide explains why changing wages can affect more than payroll taxes. Use the relevant year's rules and the owner's full return.

Establish the effective date and ordinary filing window

The Form 2553 instructions generally permit filing during the tax year before the election is to take effect, or no more than two months and 15 days after the beginning of the effective tax year. Confirm the actual tax-year start and applicable deadline adjustments. A new entity's formation date alone may not establish the correct start; collect its shareholder, asset-acquisition, and business-start facts.

If the filing may be late, review the relief route separately

The IRS late-election relief guidance describes conditional procedures, including different requirements when entity-classification relief is also needed. An intended effective date, reasonable-cause explanation, consistent reporting, and the other applicable requirements require factual review. Missing the ordinary filing window does not by itself prove that an election can be backdated or that relief is unavailable.

Use the late-election adviser checklist for that review. Keep it separate from an otherwise timely prospective election. Preserve the submitted form, consents, delivery evidence, and IRS response, and assign follow-up responsibility rather than treating a transmission receipt as final acceptance.

Confirm an operating plan before proceeding

Decide who runs payroll, who reconciles owner payments, who prepares the entity and shareholder returns, and who reviews state treatment. The payroll-provider versus tax-adviser guide helps define the handoffs. Put recurring costs and deadlines into the comparison before signing an engagement or filing an election.

Ask the accountant to explain what changes if profits fall, owner duties change, wages increase, a new shareholder arrives, or the business is sold. Use actual alternative assumptions, not a standard percentage haircut. A conclusion to remain under the current classification, obtain more records, or revisit the election later can be a supported outcome.

What the final review file should answer

  • What is the current classification, and can the proposed entity and owners qualify?
  • What compensation evidence and profit forecast support the comparison?
  • Which taxes and incremental costs were included, and which are still unknown?
  • What effective date and filing route are supported by the records?
  • Who owns payroll, returns, state compliance, and acceptance follow-up?

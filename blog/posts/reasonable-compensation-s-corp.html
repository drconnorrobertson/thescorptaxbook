S Corporation Reasonable Compensation: Document the Work and Pay Evidence

Start a reasonable-compensation review with the owner’s actual services and payments. Describe the work, examine relevant pay evidence and reconcile payroll with distributions. A fixed salary percentage, a tax-savings goal or another company’s wage does not establish the right amount for this business.

What the IRS asks you to examine

The IRS’s S corporation compensation guidance explains wage treatment for shareholder-employee services before nonwage distributions and the authority to reclassify payments. It considers receipts generated by the owner’s services, other employees, and capital or equipment. Administrative work supporting other income-producing people or assets also matters.

The guidance lists factors including duties, time, experience, comparable pay, compensation arrangements and distribution history. Record the facts behind those factors instead of choosing a salary solely to maximize the amount called a distribution. The IRS also lists Watson among its cited compensation cases; that reference does not establish a universal salary or percentage for other businesses.

Officer status and entity classification require facts

The IRS paying-yourself guidance generally treats corporate officers as employees, with an exception for officers performing no or only minor services who neither receive nor are entitled to pay. Review services and direct or indirect payments before applying a slogan about zero salary.

Reasonable-compensation questions are not confined to S corporations. The same guidance addresses corporate officers more generally and explains that partners are not employees for their partnership distributions or guaranteed payments. Identify the actual federal tax classification before applying a payroll model.

Match pay evidence before averaging it

For each comparison, retain its publication date, occupation, geography, duties, experience level, hours and pay measure. Explain which parts of the owner’s work it actually represents. An executive salary, a part-time administrative wage and a contractor’s billing rate do not become comparable merely because all three are dollar amounts.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics OEWS calculation methodology explains that most published annual wage estimates use 2,080 hours and may not represent annual pay for a different schedule. Some occupations have only annual or only hourly estimates. Check the source’s units rather than automatically dividing every annual figure by 2,080.

A useful file explains inclusion, exclusion and adjustments. Averaging mismatched sources can hide the mismatch. Keep benefits, bonus terms and other compensation components clear, and document the reviewer’s conclusion separately from the reference data.

Original fictional task ledger: remove overlapping hours

Assume an invented owner’s worksheet has three task categories and 48 working weeks. Four of the eight management hours are the same four hours already included in administration. The hourly amounts below are invented arithmetic inputs, not actual BLS observations, market rates or recommended pay. No task match or compensation conclusion is approved by this exercise.

The listed hours total 40 per week. Removing four duplicate hours leaves 36 unique hours, or 1,728 hours over 48 weeks. The arithmetic is 24 × 48 × $60 = $69,120; 8 × 48 × $25 = $9,600; and 4 × 48 × $45 = $8,640. The sum is $87,360.

Using all eight management hours would produce $96,000, including $8,640 assigned to time already counted in administration. This comparison shows the effect of duplicate hours under the invented inputs. It does not determine a reasonable salary, compensation range, tax saving or safe harbor.

Actual annual work, duties and compensation evidence could produce a different analysis. The model omits bonus, benefits, overtime treatment, payroll taxes and all other compensation adjustments. Fixing the overlap alone does not validate the hourly inputs or the task categories.

Download the three fictional task records (CSV). Listed hours, overlap, unique hours, weeks and arithmetic values have separate fields. The approved-compensation field is blank.

Connect the conclusion to actual payroll and transfers

After the evidence review, reconcile the approved treatment with payroll reports, payment dates and distributions. Explain material differences and changes during the year. A spreadsheet wage estimate is not a payroll filing or proof that deposits were timely.

Use the payroll and self-employment tax comparison to examine both sides of payroll cost, and the QBI worksheet for the shareholder deduction. Keep those calculations separate from deciding what the services support.

Review penalties by their actual requirements

The IRS failure-to-deposit guidance ties the penalty to the deposit facts and lateness. It expressly explains that the listed lateness percentages are not added together. A list of possible penalties is not a calculation of the amount due.

Section 6672 addresses willful failures by persons required to collect, account for and pay over the relevant tax. It is not an automatic 100% addition to every employer payroll-tax amount. Determine the tax, responsible-person facts and applicable requirements before estimating exposure. No audit-cost multiplier is assumed here.

Prepare a reviewable evidence record

Download a blank compensation review record (CSV). Record actual duties and nonoverlapping time, the reference source and its units, adjustments, payroll and transfer facts, open questions and the reviewer’s conclusion. Keep unsupported figures unresolved.

The companion reasonable-compensation file guide explains the supporting records to retain. Refresh the analysis when the owner’s role, hours, staffing or revenue sources change. Documentation makes the conclusion reviewable; it does not promise IRS acceptance.

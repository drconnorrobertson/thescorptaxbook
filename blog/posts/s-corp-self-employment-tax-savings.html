S Corp Payroll vs Self-Employment Tax: A 2026 Worked Example

An S-corporation comparison needs more than multiplying distributions by 15.3%. Use the correct year, the owner's other wages, the Social Security limit, both sides of payroll and the same starting business profit. Then evaluate income taxes and operating costs separately.

This original worked example compares regular Social Security and Medicare amounts under stated assumptions. It is fictional, not a client result, a recommended salary or a promise of annual savings.

The 2026 inputs

The Social Security Administration's 2026 fact sheet lists a $184,500 Social Security taxable maximum; $176,100 is the 2025 figure. The 2026 Schedule SE instructions and Schedule SE form provide the current self-employment calculation and coordination with wages.

For this example, assume a single filer with no other wages or self-employment earnings, no special computation method, and no Additional Medicare Tax. Start with $200,000 of business profit before owner salary and employer payroll taxes. For the S-corporation column, assume an eligible election and an $80,000 salary solely to illustrate the calculation; this article does not establish that salary as reasonable for any actual role.

Column one: the simplified sole-proprietor calculation

  1. Net earnings under the example's standard Schedule SE calculation: $200,000 × 92.35% = $184,700.
  2. Social Security portion: the smaller of $184,700 or the $184,500 limit, multiplied by 12.4% = $22,878.
  3. Regular Medicare portion: $184,700 × 2.9% = $5,356.30.
  4. Total of these two components: $22,878 + $5,356.30 = $28,234.30.

This is not a calculation of all taxes on the business or owner. Different wages, filing facts or exceptions can change the result. The income-tax treatment of the deductible portion of self-employment tax belongs in a separate income-tax comparison, not as a dollar-for-dollar reduction of this tax total.

Column two: include employer and employee payroll

In the fictional S-corporation column, the assumed $80,000 wage is below the 2026 Social Security limit. Regular Social Security and Medicare are $6,120 on the employer side and $6,120 on the employee side, totaling $12,240. This limited illustration excludes unemployment taxes and the other costs listed below.

Keep the business-profit comparison consistent. After the assumed salary and employer Social Security/Medicare cost, the example has $200,000 − $80,000 − $6,120 = $113,880 remaining before other omitted items. Calling the remaining amount $120,000 would omit the employer payroll cost. This is not a statement that the entire $113,880 is available or appropriate for distribution.

The two regular-tax totals differ by $28,234.30 − $12,240 = $15,994.30 under these assumptions. That difference is not net election savings. Income taxes, QBI effects, benefits, state rules, payroll service, return preparation, bookkeeping, unemployment taxes and other facts have not been modeled.

Reasonable compensation is a separate determination

The IRS shareholder-compensation guidance explains reasonable compensation for services and the possible reclassification of other payments as wages. A distribution label does not establish the treatment of a payment. Do not select a salary merely to reproduce the illustration's difference.

Prepare the owner's duties, time, responsibilities and supporting compensation information for review. If the adviser changes the wage assumption, rerun both the employer cost and the remaining business result. Use the reasonable-compensation workpaper to organize the support.

Finish the full decision before considering an election

  • Confirm the current tax classification, eligibility and intended effective year.
  • Use the same profit and owner-work assumptions in both columns.
  • Include other wages and the relevant filing facts.
  • Model the income-tax consequences alongside the payroll comparison.
  • Obtain actual quotes for recurring and transition work rather than assuming universal fees.
  • Identify the records, payroll setup and continuing responsibilities needed to carry out the plan.

The separate LLC and S-election review guide focuses on classification, readiness and scope. This numerical illustration answers only the limited calculation shown here, not whether your business should elect S status.

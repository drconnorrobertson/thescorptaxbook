LLC and S-Corp Election: Review Classification, Costs and Readiness

An LLC and an S-corporation election describe different things. An LLC is a state-law entity; S status is a federal tax election available only when its requirements are met. A useful comparison starts with the LLC's actual ownership and tax classification, then evaluates the proposed change and the work needed to operate it.

This guide prepares an election-review memo. It supplies no universal income threshold, salary ratio, professional fee or promised saving. For the separate payroll arithmetic, see the 2026 self-employment and S-corporation payroll example.

Identify the current classification

The IRS LLC overview describes how federal classification can depend on membership and elections. A domestic LLC with two or more members generally defaults to partnership treatment; a single-member LLC generally is disregarded for income-tax purposes unless it elects corporate treatment. That shorthand does not eliminate separate employment- or excise-tax questions.

Write down the legal entity, owners, prior elections, tax year and returns actually filed. Do not assume that the word “LLC” on formation documents establishes the current federal return type, or that every LLC owner's income receives the same self-employment treatment.

Check eligibility and ownership before modeling a change

The IRS S-corporation page lists eligibility conditions, including permitted shareholders, the shareholder limit and one class of stock. Ask the adviser to review the actual owners and economic arrangements. A proposed election should not silently replace terms the owners intended to retain.

Record which requirements have been checked, who checked them and which documents remain necessary. Legal rights, state filings, existing agreements, lending terms and federal classification should be coordinated with the appropriate professionals. Filing a form is not proof that every related legal or administrative question is resolved.

Compare the full work and cost

Use written service quotes and a common forecast period. Separate work already needed under the current arrangement from work added or changed by an S election. Include the owner time and records required to maintain the proposed process.

  • Payroll: setup, scheduled runs, filings, corrections, notices and year-end reporting; identify the party responsible for each.
  • Tax preparation and advice: entity and owner returns, projections, compensation review, benefits coordination and state work.
  • Books and owner records: payment classification, shareholder records, distributions and information the preparer needs.
  • Transition: professional review, effective dates, existing reporting questions and any one-time work.
  • Exclusions: items not in the quoted scope, extra charges and the assumptions behind the quote.

Compare the combined income-tax and employment-tax result after those costs, using the same business profit and owner responsibilities. A difference in one tax component is not a conclusion about the whole arrangement. Test how the result changes when profit, compensation or service needs differ from the forecast.

A fictional scope comparison

Suppose two fictional proposals both say “S-corp support.” One includes payroll processing and the entity return. The other includes a compensation review but lists payroll and state filings as separately scoped services. Neither short label establishes an equivalent engagement.

  1. List the actual deliverables and exclusions from each proposal.
  2. Identify who will maintain records, answer payroll questions and handle notices.
  3. Ask for the missing services and costs needed to complete each option.
  4. Keep unresolved items visible in the comparison; do not enter zero merely because a quote does not mention them.

This example describes no provider, price or client outcome. The payroll-provider versus tax-adviser guide helps distinguish those roles.

Plan the election only after the review

The Form 2553 instructions address the election, shareholder consent, effective dates and relief provisions. Have the preparer identify the timing rule applicable to the actual tax year and circumstances. A generic calendar date or the assumption that late relief is automatic should not replace that review.

Ask what records must be retained to establish the election's status, which payroll and return changes follow, and who will verify completion. Keep a proposed step separate from a filed or accepted step.

Give special transactions their own analysis

If real estate, a sale, new investors, owner benefits or existing tax attributes affect the decision, list those as separate review questions. For example, the IRS like-kind exchange guidance focuses on qualifying real property held for investment or business use. Do not use the entity label alone as a blanket conclusion about a particular exchange.

End the review memo with the supported assumptions, comparison result, unresolved items and responsible advisers. The purpose is to make an informed decision about this business, not to reproduce a headline saving from an unrelated example.

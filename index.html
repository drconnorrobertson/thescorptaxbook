Intro

The S Corp Advantage

Why S Corporations have become the most popular entity choice for small business owners seeking to reduce self-employment taxes, and how this book will walk you through every strategy, compliance requirement, and planning opportunity available to S Corp shareholders.

01

S Corp Basics

What an S Corporation is, how it differs from partnerships and C Corps, and the fundamental two-layer compensation structure -- salary plus distributions -- that creates the tax savings. This chapter establishes the foundation for every strategy that follows, explaining the legal and tax framework that makes S Corps uniquely powerful for owner-operators.

02

S Corp Election

S Corp eligibility requirements -- the 100 shareholder limit, one class of stock restriction, domestic corporation only rule -- and how to navigate them. Covers filing Form 2553 with the IRS, the strict timing rules that trip up many applicants, late election relief procedures, and the step-by-step process for converting an existing LLC to S Corp tax status.

03

Reasonable Compensation

Why the IRS focuses its S Corp enforcement efforts on reasonable compensation, what "reasonable" means in practice based on court cases and IRS guidance, how to set and document a defensible salary using comparable wage data and industry benchmarks, and the real financial risks of paying yourself too little -- including reclassification penalties and back taxes.

04

Pass-Through Taxation

How S Corp income passes through to shareholders on Schedule K-1 rather than being taxed at the entity level, when distributions are tax-free versus taxable, the Accumulated Adjustments Account (AAA) and why tracking it correctly is essential, and timing strategies for distributions that minimize your overall tax liability across multiple tax years.

05

QBI Deduction

The Section 199A qualified business income deduction -- who qualifies for the full 20% deduction, the W-2 wage and unadjusted basis limitations that apply to high-income taxpayers, the specified service trade or business (SSTB) phase-out rules, and the critical interplay between your S Corp salary level and QBI optimization. Getting this balance right can be worth thousands per year.

06

Basis and Losses

Stock basis versus debt basis and why the distinction matters, annual basis adjustments for income and distributions, how losses are limited by basis (and what happens to suspended losses), the at-risk rules that layer on top, and the critical fact that personal loan guarantees do not create S Corp basis -- unlike partnerships, where they do. Missing this rule is one of the most common and costly S Corp mistakes.

07

Built-In Gains Tax

The built-in gains (BIG) tax that applies when a C Corporation converts to S Corp status -- the five-year recognition period during which appreciated assets can trigger a corporate-level tax, strategies to minimize exposure by timing asset dispositions, installment sales that spread recognition beyond the window, and why corporations that were formed as S Corps from day one avoid this issue entirely.

08

S Corp vs. LLC

A side-by-side comparison of S Corp taxation versus default LLC taxation -- when S Corp election genuinely saves money (generally when net profit significantly exceeds a reasonable salary), when LLC flexibility and simplicity wins (lower-income businesses, businesses with multiple owner classes), the hybrid LLC-taxed-as-S-Corp approach that gives you both advantages, and the break-even analysis framework to make the right call.

09

Owner Benefits

Health insurance deduction rules for S Corp shareholders who own more than 2% of the company, Health Reimbursement Arrangements (HRAs), maximizing Solo 401(k) contributions using both employee deferrals and employer profit-sharing, defined benefit pension plans for high-income owners, and other fringe benefit strategies available through S Corporations that can shelter additional income from taxation.

10

State Taxes

State-level S Corporation treatment varies dramatically and can erode federal savings if you are not careful. Covers California's 1.5% franchise tax on S Corp net income, New York's complex filing rules, the advantages of operating in no-income-tax states, composite return requirements for nonresident shareholders, and multi-state withholding obligations that catch many S Corp owners off guard.

11

Exit Strategies

How to structure an S Corp sale for maximum after-tax proceeds -- the difference between asset sales (which buyers prefer for the step-up in basis) and stock sales (which sellers prefer for capital gains treatment), the Section 338(h)(10) election that gives both parties what they want, installment sale strategies for deferring gain recognition, and the tax planning steps to take in the years leading up to a sale.

12

Advanced Planning

Entity stacking strategies that use multiple S Corps or S Corps paired with other entities for tax optimization, the interaction between S Corporation status and the Section 1202 QSBS exclusion (and why it does not work directly), when S Corps work for real estate holdings and when they create problems with 1031 exchanges, and multi-entity optimization techniques for business owners with complex structures.

13

Case Studies

Three real-world case studies that put every strategy into practice: a dentist saving $18,000 per year in self-employment tax through S Corp election with properly documented reasonable compensation, a family business succession plan using a Family Limited Partnership holding S Corp stock for estate tax efficiency, and a consultant preserving maximum QBI deductions by optimizing the salary-to-distribution ratio.

S Corp Action Plan

A step-by-step implementation roadmap that brings every chapter together -- from evaluating whether S Corp election makes sense for your business, through the election and setup process, to ongoing compliance and annual optimization. Includes checklists, decision frameworks, and guidance on when to engage a tax professional for complex situations.

S corporation implementation guide

An S election is an operating discipline, not a one-time form

The S corporation combines pass-through taxation with payroll, distribution, basis, eligibility, and filing rules that must stay coordinated. Its value depends on profit, reasonable compensation, administrative cost, state treatment, owner benefits, and the company’s future. This expanded guide focuses on the records and recurring decisions that keep an election useful and defensible.

Best for: profitable owner-operated businesses and advisors evaluating or maintaining an S corporation election.

Begin with the owner’s actual job. Document duties, hours, revenue responsibility, management scope, credentials, geography, and the cost of hiring someone else to perform comparable work. Reasonable compensation is not a percentage chosen after the return is complete; it is a fact pattern that should be reviewed before payroll and distributions drift apart.

Then build a complete cash map. Owner money may move through wages, payroll withholding, reimbursements, health-insurance premiums, retirement contributions, distributions, contributions, loan advances, loan repayments, and personal charges that need reclassification. Each path needs the right accounting and reporting. A clean monthly close is the best defense against a year-end scramble that mislabels cash.

Basis tracking must also live outside the shareholder’s memory. Stock basis changes with contributions, income, loss, deductions, and distributions. Debt basis generally requires a bona fide debt running directly from the shareholder to the corporation. Bank loans merely guaranteed by an owner do not automatically create the same result. Maintain the rollforward even in profitable years so a later loss, distribution, redemption, or sale does not require reconstruction.

Eligibility deserves its own control process. Transfers to an ineligible trust or entity, a nonresident-alien shareholder, excess shareholders, or side agreements that create unequal distribution or liquidation rights can threaten the election. Cap-table changes, buy-sells, redemptions, estate events, and financing documents should therefore require tax review before signature.

State analysis can reverse a federal headline. Some jurisdictions impose entity-level taxes or fees, do not fully recognize the federal election, require separate state elections, tax nonresident shareholders, or offer pass-through entity tax elections. Add payroll location, remote employees, sales footprint, and owner residence to the annual review so the model reflects where the business really operates.

Benefits need deliberate setup. More-than-two-percent shareholders can face special treatment for health insurance and certain fringe benefits, while retirement contribution limits interact with W-2 compensation. Accountable-plan reimbursements need a written arrangement, business substantiation, and timely return of excess advances. Fixing the payment method before year-end is usually easier than repairing reporting later.

Finally, compare the election with credible alternatives every year. Profit can fall below the level that supports added administration, growth may require an ineligible investor or a second class of stock, retained capital needs may change, or a future buyer may prefer a different structure. An election should remain because it still fits the facts, not because no one scheduled a new analysis.

Turn the annual meeting into a signed action list: confirm eligibility, approve compensation, reconcile owner accounts, update stock and debt basis, review distributions, inspect benefit reporting, model state obligations, verify estimated taxes, and record planned ownership or transaction events. Assign each item to payroll, bookkeeping, tax, legal, or the shareholder so responsibility is visible.

Calendar mechanics matter as much as the analysis. Coordinate the federal election deadline, any late-election relief, payroll registrations, state elections, unemployment accounts, estimated payments, information returns, retirement deposits, and owner health-insurance reporting. Put each obligation on a shared compliance calendar with a named preparer and reviewer.

Treat shareholder loans as real legal and financial instruments. Record the advance date, principal, interest rate, maturity, payment history, security, and business purpose. Reconcile the general ledger to the note and bank activity. If the pattern is actually a distribution or contribution, correct it before the balance becomes an unsupported plug.

Plan for change before the shareholder needs it. Disability, death, divorce, departure, gifting, estate transfers, buyouts, and a new investor can change eligibility, basis, valuation, and liquidity. A coordinated buy-sell agreement, valuation method, insurance review, and tax process reduces the chance that an urgent ownership event accidentally damages the election.

Do not ignore accumulated adjustments and distribution ordering. Historical C corporation earnings, tax-free income, nondeductible expenses, redemptions, and election changes can alter how cash is characterized. Maintain the accumulated adjustments account and related tax-basis schedules with the same care given to shareholder basis.

If the company acquires assets or another business, model purchase accounting, depreciation, payroll migration, state registrations, contracts, and ownership funding before close. The S corporation’s eligibility and one-class-of-stock rules should be reviewed alongside seller notes, rollover equity, earn-outs, and new investor rights.

When an owner leaves, reconcile wages through the departure date, final reimbursements, distributions, stock transfer documents, basis, valuation, and any redemption payment terms. Coordinate the final Schedule K-1 allocation method with the transaction documents so economics and reporting do not conflict.

Keep the analysis honest. An S corporation does not make all owner cash tax-free. Wages remain subject to payroll tax, distributions require basis, and eligibility or filing failures can jeopardize the election.

Questions readers ask before they act

When does an S election usually merit analysis?

When stable net profit exceeds a defensible owner salary by enough to cover added compliance and still produce meaningful savings.

Can a single-member LLC elect S status?

Often yes for federal tax purposes if eligible, while the underlying legal entity remains an LLC under state law.

Are distributions subject to payroll tax?

Generally not when properly treated, but wages must be reasonable and distributions require adequate stock basis.

What causes election termination risk?

Ineligible shareholders, excess shareholders, nonresident aliens, or arrangements that create a second class of stock can be significant risks.

Can losses offset other income?

Potentially, but stock or debt basis, at-risk rules, passive-activity rules, and excess-business-loss limits can restrict use.

What records should be retained?

Election acceptance, governing documents, payroll support, compensation analysis, basis schedules, distributions, loans, and state filings.
